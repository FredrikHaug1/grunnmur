# 03 Best Practice Guide for Designing a B2B Marketing Site

# ​1​ Purpose

This document defines best practices for designing a B2B marketing site that does two things well:

1. Generate qualified leads
2. Build a stronger, more memorable brand

The site should not be treated as a digital brochure. It should work as a sales experience before sales gets involved. Most B2B buyers do a large part of their research before they contact a vendor, so the website must help them understand the problem, trust the solution, and feel confident enough to take the next step.

# ​2​ Strategic Principle

The website must reduce buyer uncertainty

A good B2B marketing site answers four questions quickly:

1. Is this relevant for us?
2. Do they understand our problem?
3. Can they prove that their solution works?
4. What should I do next?

Everything on the site should support at least one of these questions.

If an element does not clarify, prove, guide, or make the brand more memorable, it should be removed or redesigned.

# ​3​ Core Insight From B2B Buyer Research

Modern B2B buyers prefer to self-educate before speaking with sales. Research from Gartner and 6sense points to the same pattern: buyers often define needs, evaluate options, and form vendor preferences before talking to a salesperson.

This has two consequences:

## ​3.1​ The site must do more of the sales job

The site must explain the offer clearly, show proof, handle objections, and create trust before the buyer converts.

This means we need:

* Clear positioning
* Use-case-specific pages
* Product screenshots and visual explanations
* Customer proof
* Expert proof
* Case studies
* Relevant calls to action
* A frictionless demo/contact flow

## ​3.2​ The site must build memory, not only capture demand

Only a small percentage of potential B2B buyers are actively in-market at any given time. The majority may not be ready to buy now, but they may buy later.

This means the site should not only optimize for “Book a demo.” It should also make us:

* Easy to understand
* Easy to remember
* Easy to trust
* Easy to associate with a specific category or problem

The goal is not only conversion. The goal is future preference.

# ​4​ What the Site Must Achieve

The marketing site should support the full buyer journey.

| **Stage** | **Buyer question** | **Site responsibility** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | “Why should I care?” | Show the problem and create urgency |
| Relevance | “Is this for a company like ours?” | Segment by use case, industry, role, or company need |
| Understanding | “What do they actually offer?” | Explain the product/service clearly |
| Trust | “Can they deliver?” | Show proof: customers, experts, results, methodology |
| Evaluation | “How does this compare to alternatives?” | Explain differentiation and reduce perceived risk |
| Conversion | “What should I do next?” | Make demo/contact/trial/resource access simple |
| Brand memory | “Will I remember them later?” | Use distinctive visual and verbal identity |

# 5 Positioning and Messaging

## 5.1 The hero section must be brutally clear

The top of the homepage must answer three questions within seconds:

1. Who is this for?
2. What problem do we solve?
3. What outcome do we create?

Avoid vague statements such as:

“Empowering organizations through next-generation learning solutions.”

Use concrete statements instead:

“Build company-wide competence programs that employees actually complete — with Norwegian experts, structured learning paths, and measurable business impact.”

The exact wording can be refined, but the principle is important: the hero should be specific, not generic.

## 5.2 Lead with the customer’s problem, not our product category

B2B buyers rarely wake up thinking, “We need a learning platform.” They are more likely thinking:

* “Our leaders need a common language.”
* “We need to roll out AI competence.”
* “Employees are not completing training.”
* “We need risk-based compliance training.”
* “Our LMS is not creating business value.”
* “We need to prove that learning supports strategy.”

The site should organize messaging around these problems.

## 5.3 Use outcome-driven messaging

Good B2B copy connects features to business outcomes.

| **Weak** | **Better** |
| --- | --- |
| “Access to 500+ courses” | “Give employees relevant training tied to business priorities” |
| “Video-based learning platform” | “Deliver structured learning programs employees can complete alongside work” |
| “Expert-led courses” | “Build shared understanding with trusted Norwegian experts” |
| “Analytics dashboard” | “See who is learning, where progress stops, and which programs need follow-up” |

## 5.4 Avoid generic SaaS language

Avoid phrases like:

* Empower
* Unlock potential
* Seamless
* Next-generation
* Scalable solution
* Transform your workforce
* All-in-one platform
* Future-ready organization
* AI driven

These can work only when backed by specific explanations. As standalone claims, they sound like every other B2B site.

# 6 Information Architecture

The site should be structured around how buyers think, not how we are thinking about our product and services.

## 6.1 Suggested top-level navigation

A strong B2B marketing site should include:

1. Solutions
2. Platform
3. Programs / Content
4. Customers
5. Resources / Insights
6. About
7. Book a demo / Contact us

Right now  “Solutions” will be the most important section.

## 6.2 Suggested  solution pages

Instead of only having one generic product page, create pages for specific buying situations.

Examples:

* Leadership development
* AI competence
* Compliance and risk training
* Project management
* Onboarding
* Team development
* Strategic competence development
* Learning programs for distributed organizations

Each solution page should have its own message, proof, examples, and CTA.

## 6.3 Suggested  page types

| **Page type** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| Homepage | Position the company and route visitors to the right path |
| Solution pages | Match specific customer needs and increase relevance |
| Platform page | Explain the product experience and functionality |
| Content / programs page | Show the quality and breadth of learning content |
| Expert page | Build trust in the people behind the content |
| Customer stories | Prove business value |
| Methodology page | Explain how our approach creates transfer and impact |
| Resources | Build authority and support long buying journeys |
| Demo/contact page | Convert demand into meetings |

# 7 Homepage Best Practice Structure

The homepage should not try to say everything. It should create clarity, trust, and direction.

Recommended homepage flow

## 7.1 Hero

Purpose: Make the value proposition immediately clear.

Should include:

* Specific headline
* Short supporting copy
* Primary CTA
* Secondary CTA
* Visual proof: product screenshot, program example, expert image, or model

Example CTA structure:

* Primary: Book a demo
* Secondary: Explore learning programs or See how it works

## 7.2 Problem section

Purpose: Show that we understand the buyer’s reality.

Example themes:

* Employees have too little time for learning
* Training is often disconnected from strategy
* Course libraries alone do not create behavior change
* Companies struggle to document learning impact
* Managers need a shared language and practical tools

## 7.3 Solution overview

Purpose: Explain what we offer in simple terms.

This should answer:

* What is the platform?
* What services/support are included?
* What kind of content/programs do we offer?
* How does the customer get started?

## 7.4 Use-case routing

Purpose: Help different buyers find themselves.

Examples:

* Develop better leaders
* Roll out AI competence
* Improve compliance training
* Build project management skills
* Create a shared language across teams

## 7.5 Product proof

Purpose: Make the solution concrete.

Should include:

* Screenshots
* Learning journey examples
* Dashboard/reporting examples
* Example program structure
* Short product walkthrough or interactive demo if possible

## 7.6 Expert and content proof

Purpose: Build trust in the learning quality.

Should include:

* Expert portraits
* Credentials
* Course/program examples
* Short clips or previews
* Explanation of how content is developed

## 7.7 Customer proof

Purpose: Reduce perceived risk.

Should include:

* Customer logos
* Short testimonials
* Case study snippets
* Relevant metrics where available
* Industry-specific examples

## 7.8 Methodology

Purpose: Explain why our approach works.

This is especially important if we want to differentiate from generic course libraries.

Potential message:

“We help companies move from individual course consumption to structured competence development.”

Could include a simple model:

‌

1. Define business-critical competence
2. Build structured learning paths
3. Activate employees and managers
4. Support transfer to work
5. Measure participation, progress, and impact
6. Final CTA

Purpose: Make the next step obvious.

Use a direct CTA and reduce friction.

Example:

“Want to see how this could work in your organization?”

CTA:

* Book a demo
* Talk to us about your competence goals

# 8 Solution Page Best Practice

Solution pages are critical because they create relevance. A generic homepage rarely converts all audiences equally well.

Recommended structure for each solution page

## 8.1 Specific hero

The hero must name the exact problem and audience.

Example:

“Leadership development that creates a shared management language across the organization.”

## 8.2 Buyer pain points

Show that we understand the situation.

Example:

* Managers solve problems differently
* Leadership training is often fragmented
* New managers lack practical tools
* Training is not followed up in daily work

## 8.3 Our approach

Explain the solution clearly.

Include:

* Program structure
* Content format
* Expert involvement
* Manager/team activation
* Platform support
* Reporting/follow-up

## 8.4 What the customer gets

Be concrete.

Examples:

* Learning path
* Expert-led modules
* Reflection exercises
* Team discussions
* Templates/tools
* Manager follow-up
* Progress reporting

## 8.5 Proof

Add proof close to the claims.

Use:

* Relevant customer logos
* Case study
* Expert credibility
* Example course/module
* Screenshot
* Testimonial

## 8.6 CTA

Use a CTA that fits the level of intent.

Examples:

* Book a demo
* See example program
* Talk to us about leadership development

# 9 Visual Design Principles

## 9.1 Images are not decoration

Images should do at least one of four jobs:

1. Explain the solution
2. Prove that we can deliver
3. Humanize the brand
4. Make us memorable

If an image does none of these, it should not be used.

## 9.2 Prioritize informative visuals

For a B2B learning/competence site, the most valuable visuals are:

| **Visual type** | **Why it matters** |
| --- | --- |
| Product screenshots | Makes the platform real |
| Program examples | Shows what customers actually buy |
| Expert portraits | Builds trust in content quality |
| Learning journey diagrams | Explains the method |
| Customer case visuals | Connects solution to results |
| Simple models/frameworks | Helps buyers understand our point of view |
| Short video/demo thumbnails | Makes the offer easier to grasp |

## 9.3 Avoid generic stock photos

Generic stock photos are usually weak in B2B because they do not communicate anything specific.

Avoid:

* Smiling people around laptops
* Handshakes
* Abstract business meetings
* Generic office photography
* Random “people learning” images
* Overused SaaS illustrations

If we use people photography, it should feel real, specific, and connected to our offer.

Better alternatives:

* Real experts
* Real course production
* Real platform screenshots
* Real learning situations
* Customer environments, if available
* Custom illustrations based on our methodology

## 9.4 Build a distinctive visual system

The site should not look like a generic SaaS template.

We should define:

* Color palette
* Typography
* Icon style
* Illustration style
* Photography style
* Screenshot treatment
* Motion principles
* Layout rhythm
* CTA styling
* Data visualization style

The goal is recognition. A visitor should remember us visually after leaving the site.

## 9.5 Use motion carefully

Motion can improve the experience when it helps explain something.

Good uses of motion:

* Showing a learning journey
* Revealing steps in a process
* Demonstrating platform flow
* Drawing attention to key proof
* Making transitions feel smooth

Bad uses of motion:

* Decorative animations with no meaning
* Slow scroll effects that hide content
* Heavy video backgrounds
* Interactions that reduce accessibility
* Effects that slow down the page

Motion should support clarity, not compete with it.

# 10 Product and Service Explanation

## 10.1 Make the invisible visible

B2B services are often abstract. Learning, competence development, and organizational change can be hard to evaluate from text alone.

We need to show what the customer actually gets.

Examples:

* Example learning path
* Example course page
* Example admin dashboard
* Example reporting view
* Example implementation timeline
* Example manager follow-up flow
* Example blended learning program
* Example communication plan for employees

## 10.2 Use “show, then tell”

Instead of writing long explanations first, lead with a visual example and explain it.

Example structure:

1. Screenshot or diagram
2. Short heading
3. One-sentence explanation
4. Supporting bullets
5. CTA or link to learn more

## 10.3 Explain the difference between platform, content, and support

Buyers need to understand what type of solution this is.

We should clearly separate:

| **Component** | **What it means** |
| --- | --- |
| Platform | The technology used to deliver and follow up learning |
| Content | Courses, programs, experts, videos, tools, templates |
| Advisory/support | Help with structure, implementation, communication, reporting |
| Methodology | The principles behind effective competence development |

This is important because our differentiation may not be only the platform. It may be the combination of platform, content, experts, and implementation support.

# 11 Trust and Proof Architecture

## 11.1 B2B buyers need risk reduction

A B2B buyer is not only asking, “Is this good?” They are also asking:

* “Will this work in our organization?”
* “Will employees use it?”
* “Will leadership support it?”
* “Can I defend this purchase internally?”
* “Will this supplier be reliable?”
* “Is the content credible?”
* “Will this create measurable value?”

The site must reduce these risks.

## 11.2 Types of proof we should use

| **Proof type** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| Customer logos | Shows market trust |
| Case studies | Shows real-world value |
| Testimonials | Adds human credibility |
| Expert profiles | Proves content quality |
| Metrics | Makes impact concrete |
| Product screenshots | Proves the solution exists |
| Methodology | Shows that our approach is intentional |
| Security/privacy information | Reduces IT/procurement concerns |
| Integration information | Reduces implementation risk |

## 11.3 Place proof near claims

Do not collect all proof at the bottom of the page. Proof should appear close to the claims it supports.

Example:

If we claim:

“Employees are more likely to complete structured programs.”

Then nearby we should show:

* Completion data
* Program example
* Customer quote
* Case study
* Method explanation

If we claim:

“We work with leading Norwegian experts.”

Then nearby we should show:

* Expert portraits
* Names
* Credentials
* Course examples
* Short video clips

## 11.4 Make customer stories more useful

Weak case study:

“Customer X uses our platform and is very satisfied.”

Strong case study:

* Customer situation
* Problem
* Why they chose us
* What was implemented
* How employees used it
* What changed
* Results
* Quote
* Relevant visuals

# 12 Conversion Design

## 12.1 A lead is not the same as a meeting

The site should not only optimize for form submissions. It should optimize for qualified conversations and pipeline.

Important conversion metrics:

* Demo form starts
* Demo form completions
* Meetings booked
* Meetings attended
* Qualified opportunities
* Pipeline generated
* Closed-won revenue
* Conversion by traffic source
* Conversion by page type
* Conversion by ICP segment

## 12.2 Reduce friction in the demo flow

The demo/contact flow should be simple.

Recommended principles:

* Ask only for necessary information
* Avoid long forms
* Make the next step clear
* Allow direct meeting booking where possible
* Route leads quickly
* Confirm what happens after submission
* Use different CTAs for different intent levels

## 12.3 Use multiple CTA levels

Not every visitor is ready to book a demo. We should offer different next steps.

| **ntent level** | **CTA examples** |
| --- | --- |
| High intent | Book a demo, Talk to sales |
| Medium intent | See example program, Watch product walkthrough |
| Early intent | Read guide, Download checklist, Explore resources |
| Existing customer/partner | Contact customer success, Explore programs |

The primary CTA should be consistent, but secondary CTAs should support buyers who are still researching.

## 11.4 CTA copy should be specific

Avoid vague CTAs like:

* Learn more
* Submit
* Get started

Better options:

* Book a demo
* See the platform
* Explore leadership programs
* Talk to us about AI training
* Get an example learning path
* See how it works

# 12​ Signature Diagnostic Tools

A B2B marketing site should not only capture existing demand through demo forms. It should also create value before the buyer speaks with sales.

One effective way to do this is through a **signature diagnostic tool**: an interactive tool that gives the visitor a company-specific insight, benchmark, assessment, or recommendation.

For us, this means building a **Company Competence Analysis Tool** where the visitor enters an organization number and/or name, receives a preliminary company-specific analysis based on public company data and industry benchmarks, and can request the full report by entering a verified work email.

This should be treated as a **product experience**, not as a traditional gated PDF.

## ​12.1​ Why this matters

Most B2B buyers are not ready to book a demo when they first visit a website. A diagnostic tool gives them a lower-friction way to engage while still creating strong buying signals.

The tool should help us:

* Create real value before asking for a meeting
* Demonstrate our point of view on strategic competence development
* Connect learning and competence development to business performance
* Make the website more useful and memorable
* Generate better-qualified leads than generic content downloads
* Give sales a more relevant reason to follow up

The goal is not only to collect email addresses. The goal is to make the buyer think:

"This company understands our business and can help us identify where competence development could create measurable value."

## ​12.2​ Core principle

A signature diagnostic tool must deliver enough value before the gate to earn the right to ask for contact information.

Do not ask for an email too early. The visitor should first see evidence that the output is specific to their company.

The preview should show:

* Company name
* Industry or company category
* 2-3 high-level observations
* One example competence opportunity
* A clear preview of what is included in the full report

The full report can then be sent by email after the visitor enters a work email.

## 12.3​ Site placement  

The diagnostic tool should be easy to find, but it should not replace the main product story.

Recommended placement:

‌

| **Location** | **Recommended use** |
| --- | --- |
| Main navigation | Add as a visible item or secondary CTA, for example "Free analysis" |
| Homepage hero | Use as secondary CTA next to "Book a demo" |
| Homepage body | Add a dedicated section after the problem or solution overview |
| Solution pages | Use contextual CTAs based on the page topic |
| Customer stories | Invite visitors to see where their company may have similar opportunities |
| Resources section | List under "Tools" or "Analysis" |
| Campaign landing pages | Use as the primary conversion offer for cold traffic |
| ABM pages | Use company-specific or industry-specific variants for strategic accounts |

Recommended CTA hierarchy:

‌

* For warm/high-intent visitors: **Book a demo** should remain primary.
* For cold/early-stage visitors: **Get a free competence analysis** can be primary.
* On solution pages: use contextual CTA copy tied to the specific business problem.

## ​12.4​ CTA examples  

Use specific CTA language that communicates value.

Good CTA examples:

* Get a free competence analysis
* Analyze your company
* Find competence opportunities
* See where training can improve margins
* Generate your company report
* Start competence scan

Avoid vague CTA language:

* Submit
* Learn more
* Download
* Get started

## ​12.5​ Design requirements

The diagnostic tool should feel like a serious product experience.

It should have:

* A dedicated landing page
* A clear explanation of how the analysis works
* A simple organization-number input flow
* A meaningful preview before email capture
* A transparent explanation of data sources and assumptions
* Clear disclaimers about limitations
* Strong visual presentation of findings
* A frictionless email capture step
* CRM integration for sales follow-up

‌

The tool should not feel like a marketing trick. It must deliver real insight.

## ​12.6​ Trust requirements

Because the tool uses public company data, benchmark data, and AI-generated interpretation, trust is critical.

The page must clearly explain:

1. What data sources are used
2. What the analysis can and cannot conclude
3. That the report identifies potential opportunity areas, not guaranteed financial outcomes
4. Why a work email is required
5. How the user may be contacted after requesting the report
6. How data is stored and handled

Recommended disclaimer language:

The analysis is based on publicly available company data, industry benchmarks, and predefined competence opportunity models. It is intended as a strategic starting point, not as financial advice. The report identifies potential areas worth investigating and does not guarantee margin improvement.

## 12.7​ Sales follow-up principle  

The diagnostic tool should create a more relevant sales conversation than a generic content download.

Sales should not follow up with:

"I saw you downloaded a report."

Sales should follow up with:

"I saw that you generated a competence analysis for your company. The analysis pointed to leadership routines and project execution as potential areas to investigate. Would it be useful to discuss how those areas could be turned into a structured learning program?"

This requires the tool to send structured insight to CRM, not only contact information.

## ​12.8​ CRM data requirements  

At minimum, the lead record should include:

* Company name
* Organization number
* Industry code/category
* Company size indicators where available
* Financial indicators where available
* Benchmark comparison status
* Main competence opportunity areas
* Email address
* Domain match status
* Source/UTM
* Analysis generated date
* Report sent status
* Recommended sales follow-up angle

## ​12.9​ Measurement

The tool should be measured as both a conversion asset and a brand-building experience.

Primary metrics:

* Analysis starts
* Successful company lookups
* Preview-to-email conversion rate
* Full reports sent
* Qualified leads created
* Sales accepted leads
* Meetings booked
* Opportunities created
* Pipeline generated
* Closed-won revenue

Quality metrics:

* ICP fit
* Domain match rate
* Company size
* Industry relevance
* Sales reply rate
* Meeting conversion rate
* Opportunity conversion rate

Experience metrics:

* Drop-off at organization-number step
* Drop-off after preview
* Report open rate
* CTA click-through from report
* Return visits from analyzed companies

## ​12.10​ Best practice checklist  

Before launching or changing a diagnostic tool, confirm:

* The tool has a dedicated landing page
* The value proposition is clear within 5-10 seconds
* The user gets meaningful value before email capture
* The output is company-specific, not generic
* Data sources and limitations are transparent
* The analysis avoids unsupported financial claims
* The full report is useful enough to justify the email gate
* Work email validation is helpful but not too aggressive
* CRM receives structured lead context
* Sales has recommended follow-up language
* Privacy and marketing-consent language has been reviewed
* Performance, security, and error handling are tested
* Success is measured by lead quality and pipeline, not only form submissions

## ​12.11​ Final principle  

A signature diagnostic tool should make our core belief tangible:

Competence development should not be random course consumption. It should be connected to business goals, operational performance, and measurable improvement opportunities.

# 13 Brand Building

## 13.1 The site should make us memorable

Lead generation captures existing demand. Brand building creates future demand.

The site should help visitors remember:

* What category we belong to
* What problem we solve
* What makes us different
* What we believe about learning
* What we look and sound like

## 13.2 Build a clear point of view

The site should not only describe products. It should communicate a belief.

Potential point of view:

“Companies do not create competence by giving employees access to a course library. They create competence by building structured learning experiences connected to real work, shared priorities, and business goals.”

This type of point of view helps differentiate us from generic learning platforms.

## 13.3 Use distinctive assets

Distinctive assets can include:

* A recognizable color system
* A strong typography style
* A named methodology
* A visual model
* A consistent illustration style
* Expert-led imagery
* Specific language patterns
* A memorable tagline or concept

The goal is that people recognize us even before they see the logo.

# 14 Content Strategy

## 14.1 The site should support long buying journeys

B2B buyers may return several times before contacting us. The site should give them useful content at different levels of readiness.

‌

Suggested content types:

| **Content type** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| Strategic guides | Build authority |
| Checklists | Help buyers act |
| Templates | Provide practical value |
| Case studies | Reduce risk |
| Webinars | Build trust and capture leads |
| Benchmark reports | Create urgency |
| Expert articles | Strengthen thought leadership |
| Program examples | Make the offer concrete |

## 14.2 Content should connect to commercial pages

Resources should not live in a separate universe. They should connect naturally to solution pages and CTAs.

Example:

An article about leadership development should link to:

* Leadership development solution page
* Relevant customer case
* Example leadership program
* Demo CTA

## 14.3 Use ungated and gated content intentionally

Do not gate everything.

Recommended approach:

| **Content** | **Gate?** |
| --- | --- |
| Blog articles | Usually no |
| Short guides | Usually no |
| Templates/checklists | Sometimes |
| Webinars | Often yes |
| Benchmark reports | Often yes |
| High-value tools | Often yes |
| Product demo | No, unless booking is required |

Over-gating can reduce trust and reach. Use gating when the value is high enough and the buyer intent is meaningful.

# 15 Technical and UX Requirements

## 15.1 Performance matters

Large images, videos, and animations can make the site slower. A slow B2B site creates friction and can reduce trust.

Best practices:

* Optimize all images
* Use modern formats where possible
* Avoid unnecessary background video
* Lazy-load media below the fold
* Keep hero media lightweight
* Monitor Core Web Vitals
* Test mobile performance, not only desktop

## 15.2 Mobile still matters in B2B

Many B2B decisions happen on desktop, but discovery often happens on mobile. Executives may open links from LinkedIn, newsletters, or email on their phone.

Mobile pages must have:

* Clear headline
* Fast loading
* Readable text
* Simple navigation
* Tap-friendly CTAs
* No broken layouts
* No tiny screenshots without zoom alternatives

## 15.3 Accessibility is part of quality

Accessibility supports trust, usability, and professionalism.

Minimum requirements:

* Sufficient color contrast
* Keyboard navigation
* Alt text for meaningful images
* Clear focus states
* Semantic headings
* Descriptive link text
* Captions for video
* Avoid motion that cannot be paused

# 16 Measurement Framework

## 16.1 Measure both demand capture and brand building

A B2B marketing site should not be judged only by immediate form conversions.

We should measure:

Demand capture metrics

* Demo requests
* Contact requests
* Meeting bookings
* Qualified leads
* Opportunities
* Pipeline
* Closed-won revenue
* Conversion by page
* Conversion by source
* Conversion by segment

Buying intent metrics

* Return visits from target accounts
* Visits to pricing/contact/demo pages
* Case study views
* Product page engagement
* Resource downloads
* Webinar registrations
* Scroll depth on solution pages
* CTA clicks by use case

‌

Brand-building metrics

* Direct traffic
* Branded search
* Returning visitors
* Share of search
* LinkedIn engagement from target audience
* Newsletter signups
* Time spent with thought-leadership content
* Increase in organic traffic to category/problem pages

## 16.2 Measure quality, not only quantity

A high number of leads is not valuable if they are poor-fit leads.

We should track:

* ICP fit
* Company size
* Industry
* Role/title
* Use case
* Sales acceptance rate
* Opportunity conversion rate
* Average deal size
* Sales cycle length
* Closed-won rate

## 16.3 Create a page-level dashboard

Each important page should have a clear job.

Example:

| **Page** | **Primary job** | **Main KPI** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homepage | Route and position | CTA clicks, solution-page visits |
| Solution page | Create relevance | Demo clicks, scroll depth, case clicks |
| Platform page | Explain product | Demo clicks, product video views |
| Case study | Build trust | Assisted conversions, CTA clicks |
| Resource page | Capture early demand | Downloads, newsletter signups |
| Demo page | Convert | Meeting bookings, completion rate |

# 17 Design Inspiration Principles

Based on award-winning B2B and digital marketing examples, we should draw inspiration from several directions.

## 17.1 Brand system as experience

Examples like Dropbox Brand and Docusign’s rebrand experience show that B2B brands can feel distinctive, interactive, and premium.

Principle:

Do not only present the brand. Let users experience it.

For us, this could mean:

* A strong visual system for competence development
* Recognizable models and frameworks
* Expert-led visual identity
* Interactive program examples
* Motion that explains learning journeys

## 17.2 Product storytelling as editorial experience

# 18 Practical Design Checklist

Use this checklist when reviewing any page.

Clarity

* Can a visitor understand the page within 5–10 seconds?
* Is the target audience clear?
* Is the problem clearly stated?
* Is the outcome concrete?
* Is the language specific rather than generic?

Relevance

* Does the page speak to a specific use case, role, industry, or need?
* Does the visitor quickly understand whether this is relevant for them?
* Are examples tailored to the page topic?
* Are CTAs aligned with the visitor’s likely intent?

Proof

* Are claims supported by evidence?
* Are customer logos or cases relevant?
* Are expert credentials visible?
* Are product screenshots or examples included?
* Is proof placed close to the claim it supports?

Visuals

* Does each image explain, prove, humanize, or create memory?
* Are generic stock photos avoided?
* Are screenshots clear and readable?
* Is the visual style consistent?
* Does the page feel distinctive?

Conversion

* Is there one clear primary CTA?
* Is there a secondary CTA for lower-intent visitors?
* Is the form short enough?
* Is the next step after conversion clear?
* Are demo/contact CTAs repeated at natural decision points?

Brand

* Does the page express a clear point of view?
* Does it sound like us?
* Does it look recognizably ours?
* Does it avoid generic SaaS clichés?
* Would a visitor remember anything specific after leaving?

Technical UX

* Does the page load quickly?
* Does it work well on mobile?
* Are images optimized?
* Is the page accessible?
* Are animations useful and non-disruptive?

# 21 Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1:** Designing for ourselves, not the buyer

Internal categories are rarely the same as buyer categories. Buyers care about their problem, not our organizational structure.

**Mistake 2**: Using vague positioning

If competitors could use the same headline, it is too generic.

**Mistake 3:** Treating visuals as decoration

Every visual should explain, prove, humanize, or make the brand memorable.

**Mistake 4:** Hiding the product

If visitors cannot see the product, program structure, or delivery model, they may assume the offer is vague or immature.

**Mistake 5:** Saving proof for the bottom of the page

Proof should appear where doubt appears.

**Mistake 6:** Optimizing only for form fills

The real goal is qualified meetings, pipeline, and revenue.

**Mistake 7:** Looking like every other SaaS company

A professional site is not enough. It must also be distinctive.

# 22 Evidence Base Behind These Recommendations

The recommendations are based on patterns from B2B buyer research, UX research, conversion case studies, and award-winning B2B/digital experiences.

Buyer behavior research

* Gartner research indicates that many B2B buyers prefer self-directed buying experiences and that inconsistency between website information and sales conversations can reduce trust.
* 6sense buyer research shows that B2B buyers often complete a large part of the buying journey before first contact with sales.
* The “95:5 rule” from B2B marketing research argues that most potential buyers are not in-market right now, so brand memory matters in addition to lead capture.

UX and credibility research

* Nielsen Norman Group’s eye-tracking research shows that users pay attention to relevant, information-rich images but often ignore decorative or generic imagery.
* Stanford Web Credibility research shows that visual design quality influences perceived credibility.
* Web performance research from Google and HTTP Archive shows that heavy images and media can harm load time and user experience.

Conversion and website case studies

* Moz increased sales and conversion through a research-driven landing page that addressed buyer questions, objections, and proof needs.
* Campaign Monitor improved landing-page conversion by matching page copy more closely to search intent.
* 6sense, Snowflake, and LaunchDarkly reported strong pipeline and meeting results from personalized B2B web experiences.
* Navattic and Chili Piper show the importance of reducing friction between demo interest and actual meeting booking.
* Mural’s website redesign case shows how product storytelling, brand experience, and self-serve conversion can improve together.

Design inspiration

* Dropbox Brand Website and Docusign Rebrand show how B2B brands can create distinctive, interactive brand experiences.
* Shopify Editions shows how product communication can become an editorial digital experience.
* Spotify Advertising’s “Spreadbeats” shows that B2B creativity can come from the buyer’s actual work context.
* KPMG’s AI-X campaign shows how personalized digital experiences can support enterprise B2B marketing and ABM.
