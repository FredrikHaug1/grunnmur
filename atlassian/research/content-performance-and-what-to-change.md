# Content performance, and what to change

**For the marketing manager · June 2026**

Synthesis of <custom data-type="smartlink" data-id="id-0">http://videocation.no</custom>  across Search Console (how content is found), Matomo (how it's read), and HubSpot (what the contact form catches). Focused on content, messaging, and the calls you own. Findings are stated plainly; interpretation is flagged \[judgement\]. Recommendations are framed as decisions with trade-offs, not instructions.

# **The content verdict**

Our content earns attention and then loses it. It ranks, it pulls readers, it brings real traffic — and almost none of that traffic travels any further or converts. It's also pointed at the wrong people: built, over years, for individuals learning a skill and for our own brand, not for the enterprise buyer we're now selling to. The job isn't to make more content. It's to make content that holds a reader and speaks to a buyer.

# **What works — and the lesson in it**

Our two strongest pages in search aren't catalogue pages. They're two deep blog posts — the "hersketeknikker på jobb" piece and the PowerPoint guide — and between them they carry roughly 17% of all our search traffic. They earned their rankings by being genuinely the best answer, not by existing.

‌

**\[judgement\]** That's the whole content lesson in miniature: depth won, volume is treading water. The era when publishing _more_ built authority is closing — search engines demote thin content and AI answer-engines absorb generic how-to traffic before it ever reaches us. What survives is content distinctive enough that a machine can't generate it from thin air. For us that means our behaviour-change and learning-transfer evidence, real Norwegian enterprise case studies, the Kompetansekompass data, a genuine point of view on why collective competence beats individual courses. That content does double duty — it's also exactly what the buyer responds to. The SEO fix and the B2B pivot are the same content brief.

# **The problem hiding in plain sight: nothing holds the reader**

Every blog post behaves the same way — readers arrive, read the one piece, and leave. Exit rates sit at **90–98%**. And this isn't just the consumer posts. The _on-strategy_ content — the customer success stories (Bauda, Hero Norge), the kollektiv-kompetanse piece — pulls strong readership and then loses every single reader. We're spending our best strategic content as a one-night stand.

‌

**The decision:** what is the blog _for_? If it's brand awareness, 90% exit is acceptable and the metric is reach. If it's meant to move readers toward the product or a conversation, it's failing, and the fix is structural — related content, a relevant next step, a reason to stay — not more posts. Right now it's implicitly the second while performing as the first.

# **The buyer-vocabulary gap**

In search, the language of our new positioning — `kompetanseutvikling for ansatte`, `kompetanseplattform`, `opplæring av ansatte`, `internopplæring`, `læringskultur`, `kollektiv kompetanse` — barely registers. Crucially, it's _not_ that we rank badly for these terms. It's that there's almost no search demand reaching us on them at all. So this is a build-from-scratch content roadmap aimed at _creating_ demand, not a rewrite to capture existing demand. It pays back over quarters.

‌

**The decision:** how much of the content calendar goes to demand we don't have yet (buyer-vocabulary content) versus demand we already capture (individual-skill content that brings the wrong audience)? That ratio is your single biggest content lever.

# **The two viral consumer posts**

The “Hersketeknikker” and “PowerPoint” posts bring a large, engaged audience — and it's the wrong audience for enterprise sales. They're also a real brand-awareness surface.

‌

**The decision:** keep, repurpose, or let decay. Keeping them as-is sustains traffic that doesn't serve the strategy. Repurposing — angling them toward the organisational/workplace lens, adding a next step — might convert some of that attention. Letting them decay frees the focus but forfeits the reach. This is worth making on purpose, because right now it's happening by neglect.

# **The catalogue, from a content-cost view**

We publish the full course catalogue, and it's expensive to produce. The data is clear that it's a weak _acquisition_ asset — 336 course pages averaging about 17 unique pageviews each over five and a half months, thin on every channel, not just search. The body of it still pulls meaningful aggregate traffic, and the `/kurs` hub itself is a workhorse, but no individual course page earns its keep as a destination.

‌

**The decision** sits partly with management (is the catalogue a sales-credibility asset?), but the content-resource question is yours: is producing every course page the best use of content capacity, or would fewer, deeper, buyer-facing assets return more? Note this is a genuine trade-off — the catalogue may prove product depth in a sales conversation even while it underperforms as content.

# **The contact form is carrying too much**

The single contact form is simultaneously our sales-lead intake, our support desk, and our cancellation channel. Of 117 submissions, only \~25–35 were real sales leads; the rest were support, cancellations, and individual queries. Even genuine buyers mostly arrive via generic `/kontakt` rather than the dedicated enterprise pages.

‌

**The decision:** the form's content and routing are a marketing-owned surface. Separating the jobs — a clear sales path distinct from support — would stop the sales signal getting buried and would let the enterprise pages host a conversion action of their own. (The technical side of this is in the dev brief.)

## The known gap you should care about most: are we measuring LinkedIn?

We're running a LinkedIn strategy to build brand awareness with HR leaders — and we currently can't tell if it's working. As a direct _click_ source, LinkedIn is tiny (118 visits in the period). But the entire point of an awareness strategy is the indirect path: someone reads a post, doesn't click, and later searches our name. That payoff lands invisibly in "brand search" and "direct traffic," unattributed.

‌

**This is the measurement gap to close first**, because it's the one place we're spending real effort blind. Practical ways in: campaign-tag every LinkedIn link so the direct clicks are at least clean; watch whether brand-search and direct volume move in step with posting activity; and over time, track whether branded search grows as the LinkedIn audience grows. None of it is perfect attribution, but all of it beats the current zero visibility.

# **On-site search, briefly**

What people type into our own search box is almost entirely individual-skill course-finding (`excel`, `copilot`, `hms`, `power bi`, `teams`, compliance topics) — and it's mostly existing logged-in users hunting the catalogue, not prospects researching. So it's a weaker buyer-intent signal than hoped, but it reinforces the picture: engagement on our properties is individual-skill shaped.

# **The shape of the content decisions**

Pulled together, the calls in front of you: (1) what the blog is _for_, and whether to fix its structural failure to retain; (2) how much content capacity goes to creating buyer demand versus capturing individual-skill demand; (3) keep / repurpose / decay on the viral consumer posts; (4) the catalogue's content-resource trade-off; (5) separating the contact form's jobs; and (6) closing the LinkedIn measurement gap so the awareness strategy stops being invisible. The data points clearly at each; the choices are judgement calls about how far and how fast.
