# The marketing site: how it's performing, and why it needs to change

‌

**For the management group · June 2026**

‌

This is the synthesis of a three-source analysis of <custom data-type="smartlink" data-id="id-0">http://videocation.no</custom>  — Google Search Console (how we're found), Matomo (what visitors do), and HubSpot (what happens to the leads). It's written to inform the decision about rebuilding the site around our enterprise positioning. Findings from the data are stated plainly; interpretation is flagged **\[judgement\]**.

# **The verdict in one paragraph**

The marketing site works — for the company we used to be. It's found by people searching our name and by individuals looking to learn a personal skill, it's anchored by a strong homepage, and it quietly serves our existing customers well. What it does _not_ do is acquire enterprise customers. Over five and a half months, the entire website produced **two** B2B customers through its own funnel. That's not a broken site. It's a site that was never built for the job we now need it to do. The gap between what the site is and where the company is going is the reason to rebuild.

# **How we know — three findings, three sources**

**We're found for the wrong things.** In search, roughly half of all clicks are people typing our own name, and almost all the rest are individuals after a single skill — PowerPoint, Excel, workplace tactics. The enterprise buyer's vocabulary — competence development, employee training, learning platforms — barely registers. We rank perfectly well for buyer terms when they're searched; the problem is that, through our site, almost nobody searches them. Two consumer blog posts alone carry about 17% of all our search traffic.

‌

**Visitors don't travel.** People arrive, often on a blog post, read the one thing, and leave — our blog posts lose 90%+ of their readers without a second click, including the posts that are _on_ our new strategy. Our enterprise pages do get visited (mostly from inside the site and from LinkedIn, not from search), but well over half of those visitors bounce. The content earns the visit; nothing catches it.

‌

**The funnel is thin top to bottom.** The genuine enterprise interest that exists squeezes through a single generic contact form that's simultaneously a sales desk, a support desk, and a cancellation desk. Of 117 form submissions, perhaps 25–35 were real sales leads. Two became customers.

‌

Four independent angles, one conclusion: **the site is a B2C-era asset that doesn't yet function as a B2B acquisition channel.**

‌

# **Why this won't fix itself — and is quietly getting worse**

**\[judgement\]** The strategy that built this site — publish a large volume of content to win search — is aging out industry-wide. Search engines now demote high-volume thin content, and AI answer-engines increasingly answer "how do I do X" without anyone clicking through. The traffic our individual-skill pages capture is exactly the kind being absorbed at the answer layer. So standing still doesn't hold the line; it erodes even the B2C base we have. The encouraging half: the move that modernises our search position — fewer, deeper, distinctively-ours assets — is the _same_ move that speaks to the enterprise buyer. Fixing the search problem and following the business strategy point in one direction.

‌

# **What this analysis does _not_ tell us**

Two honest limits, named so they don't surprise anyone later:

‌

* **The website is one channel, and currently a minor one.** Our real B2B engine is outbound sales, referrals, and expansion inside existing clients. Sparebanken Møre and SpareBank 1 in our portofolio did not arrive through a contact form. "Two from the site" is a verdict on one channel, not on Videocation's B2B sales.
* **We can't yet measure whether our LinkedIn brand-awareness strategy is working.** We're actively investing in LinkedIn to build awareness with HR leaders. The plausible payoff — someone reads a post, then later searches our name — lands invisibly in "brand search" and "direct traffic," which we can't currently attribute back to LinkedIn. **This is a measurement gap we should close deliberately**, because right now we're running a strategy we can't see the results of. (Worth noting: as a direct _click_ source, LinkedIn is tiny — 118 visits in the period — so if it's working, it's working through awareness, not clicks, which is precisely the thing we're not measuring.)

‌

# **The decisions this puts in front of management**

These are framed as choices with trade-offs, not recommendations — the calls are yours.

‌

* **How much current traffic are we willing to walk away from?** The site's existing audience is largely individuals and brand-name searchers. Rebuilding for the enterprise buyer means deprioritising content that brings real volume but the wrong people. How aggressively?

‌

* **Are we prepared to invest in _creating_ buyer demand, not just capturing it?** We rank fine for enterprise terms — there's just no search demand reaching them. Closing that is a content-and-demand-generation roadmap that pays back over quarters, not a quick SEO fix. Is there an appetite for that runway?

‌

* **What is the course catalogue for?** The data shows it's a weak acquisition asset on every channel — hundreds of pages, very little traffic each. But it may earn its keep as proof of product depth in an enterprise sales conversation. That's a strategic judgement, not a data point. Keep it as a sales-credibility asset, or invest the production resource elsewhere?

‌

* **Will we fund the measurement to know if any of this is working?** Today, finding our two web-sourced customers required reading a spreadsheet by hand. We can't see our own funnel end-to-end, and we can't see whether LinkedIn is paying off. A rebuild is the moment to fix that — or to consciously decide not to.

‌

‌

# **The bottom line**

We don't have a performance problem to tune. We have a site built for a previous business model, measured against a new one, and the measurement is unambiguous across every source we have. The rebuild isn't a refresh — it's building the acquisition channel we don't currently have. The questions above are the ones worth resolving before design starts, because they decide what we're building, not just how it looks.
